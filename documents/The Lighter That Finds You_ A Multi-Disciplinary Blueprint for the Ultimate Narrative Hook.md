- Your brain cannot ignore an unfinished story
The psychological foundation for the lighter hook rests on Bluma Zeigarnik's 1927 discovery that
interrupted tasks are recalled nearly twice as well as completed ones. Her study, inspired by a Berlin waiter
who remembered unpaid orders perfectly but forgot them after payment,  revealed that
every initiated narrative creates a "quasi-need" — a persistent cognitive tension that demands resolution.
Participants recalled interrupted tasks 90% better than completed ones;
in children, the gap widened to 80% vs 12%.
This effect powers what screenwriters call the "open loop." Neuroscientist Wolfram Schultz's reward
prediction error model (1997) explains the mechanism: dopamine neurons fire not when rewards arrive but
when rewards are anticipated.  An unresolved narrative holds dopamine elevated indefinitely.
Robinson and Berridge's distinction between "wanting" (dopamine-driven compulsion) and "liking" (opioid-
driven pleasure)  is critical here — the lighter hook must activate wanting, the itch that cannot
be scratched without scanning the QR code.

functions like hunger — a deprivation state that creates almost physical discomfort.  His most
actionable finding for the project: a small "priming dose" of information dramatically increases
curiosity far more than giving nothing at all.  The sticker must provide just enough — a
fragment that makes the gap between what you know and what you need to know feel unbearable. The
wanting will always be more intense than the having.  Brain imaging by Kang et al.

anticipation.

- Fire made us human — literally, neurologically, socially
The $LIGHTER mythology of Auauauau discovering fire 1.7 million years ago is grounded in real science,
though the timeline is debated. The earliest secure evidence of controlled fire comes from Wonderwerk
Cave, South Africa (~1 million years ago), where burned bone and ashed plant remains were found
30 meters inside the cave — too deep for lightning (Berna et al., 2012, PNAS).
Contested evidence at Chesowanja, Kenya pushes the date to ~1.42 million years.
What is certain: by the time of Homo erectus, fire had begun reshaping the human
species at every level.
Richard Wrangham's "Catching Fire" hypothesis (2009) argues that cooking was the decisive event in
human evolution  — not tool use, not language. Cooking increased caloric availability so
dramatically that it enabled the metabolic budget for larger brains  (which consume 25% of
our energy), drove the reduction of jaws, teeth, and gut size, freed hours previously spent chewing
(chimps chew 5+ hours daily), and created the hearth as the first fixed social center.  The word
"focus" literally means "hearth" in Latin  — that our term for concentrated attention
derives from the fireplace reveals how deeply fire is embedded in the architecture of human cognition.
Johannes Kepler adopted focus in 1604 for the point where light converges;  the metaphor
was irresistible.
Polly Wiessner's landmark 2014 study of Ju/'hoansi Bushmen firelight conversations (PNAS) provides the
most direct evidence of fire's social function.  Daytime talk centered on economic
matters and social sanctioning — practical, transactional. But nighttime firelit talk shifted to storytelling,
imagination, remembering distant people, healing the day's conflicts, and transmitting cultural
institutions.  Fire talk evoked "higher orders of theory of mind"  — people
imagining what others were thinking about what still others were thinking. As Wiessner wrote: "There is
something about fire in the middle of the darkness that bonds, mellows and also excites people. It's
intimate."  She called firelit stories "the original social media."

isolates.  Around the fire, narrative pours into the listener, creating immersion that reading can

focal point — and the movie theater, the television, and the smartphone all recapitulate the same
architecture: sitting in darkness, staring at light, receiving stories.

The philosophy of objects provides the theoretical spine for why a "traveling lighter" carries such weight.
Martin Heidegger's distinction between "ready-to-hand" and "present-at-hand" (Being and Time, 1927)
is the key insight. When a tool functions normally — a hammer, a lighter — it is invisible.  The
carpenter doesn't think about the hammer; she thinks through it toward the nail.
But when the tool breaks, goes missing, or malfunctions, three things happen
simultaneously: the object becomes conspicuous (suddenly visible as a thing with properties),
the entire referential network of equipment becomes visible  (the pocket, the
cigarette, the bar, the night), and the object shifts from transparent medium to opaque presence.
Loss is a revelatory event. A lost lighter becomes more meaningful than one that never left your pocket.
Walter Benjamin's concept of "aura" (The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction, 1935)
explains why a lighter that has traveled through many hands carries power that a new one cannot.
Aura emerges from three factors: physical uniqueness, temporal accretion (the passage through time,
accumulating ownership and wear), and ritual embeddedness. "Even the most perfect reproduction of a work
of art is lacking in one element: its presence in time and space, its unique existence at the place where it
happens to be."  A lighter with scratches, residue, and a mysterious sticker has an aura a factory-
new lighter never will.

The cross-cultural mythology of fire reveals a structure so consistent across unconnected civilizations that it

lighter that has been in many pockets carries the accumulated essence of every person who held it. This is
not superstition; it is a universal cognitive pattern operating in educated adults.

never build something that feels like a game. As ARG writer Sean Stewart explained: "A book has a frame
— a box. Between the covers, disbelief is suspended. An alternate reality game asks you to extend that
bubble of suspension of disbelief into your actual life."  The lighter sticker must never feel like
marketing. It must feel like a clue from within a story.

- The hook itself: seven principles and five candidate sticker texts
